Cross-border eCommerce cost

Cross-Border eCommerce Cost
and How to Reduce It

Duties, VAT, new EU charges and returns all add up. See where cross-border costs come from and which ones you can recover.

Landed cost is more than shipping and duty.

Cross-border eCommerce cost includes import duty, VAT, customs data and clearance, the new EU charges per item (the €3 duty and the €2 handling fee), and the cost of returns. Part of it can be recovered, and the rest can be planned.

Paid up front

Duties and import VAT are paid when the goods cross the border, so every order ties up cash until the sale is complete.

The rules keep changing

The EU’s €3 duty, its €2 handling fee and mandatory product identifiers are the latest changes for B2C sellers.

Part of it comes back

Duty on returned or re-exported goods can be recovered through duty drawback and Returned Goods Relief.

What makes up cross-border eCommerce cost

The cost of an order depends on where the goods are made, where they ship from and where the customer is.

The main components

  • Import duty, which depends on the customs tariff and on the value and classification of each product.
  • VAT on import in the destination market, where it applies.
  • International shipping, customs clearance and brokerage.
  • Customs data and the new EU charges: the €3 duty and the €2 handling fee, per item.
  • Returns: return shipping, handling, and the duty and VAT paid on goods that go back.
The EU changes in 2026
Since 1 July 2026, the EU applies a temporary flat customs duty of €3 per item on the customs declaration for low-value B2C consignments (€150 or less), until 1 July 2028. Since 1 November 2026, it also applies a Union handling fee of €2 per item to distance sales of any value, and product identifiers are mandatory on EU customs declarations for distance sales of imported goods with an intrinsic value of €150 or less. VAT applies to the handling fee, and the fee is non-refundable.

Where cross-border costs hide

Some costs do not show up on the invoice. They appear as cash tied up, duty paid twice or claims that were never filed.

Five hidden costs

  • Cash tied up: duties and VAT are paid up front, before the customer has kept the goods.
  • Duty paid twice: goods coming back to the market they were exported from can be charged again if relief is not claimed.
  • Lost recovery: duty paid on goods that are returned or re-exported is not refunded unless you claim it.
  • Data gaps: missing product identifiers and unmatched shipments hold up clearance and weaken claims.
  • Complexity: separate rules, systems and deadlines in the US, EU and UK.

Duty, and in the EU and the UK VAT, paid on goods that are later returned or re-exported can be recovered through duty drawback. Duty on goods that come back can be avoided through Returned Goods Relief. Shipping and return handling are not customs costs and are not recovered. The EU €3 duty per item cannot be recovered, at least until July 2028, and the €2 handling fee is non-refundable.

Since 1 July 2026, the EU applies a temporary flat customs duty of €3 per item on the customs declaration for low-value B2C consignments of €150 or less, until 1 July 2028. Since 1 November 2026, it also applies a Union handling fee of €2 per item to goods sold under distance sales arrangements, regardless of their value, including orders above €150. VAT applies to the handling fee, except in the specified circumstances in which the IOSS is used. Neither charge can be recovered: the €3 duty cannot, at least until July 2028, and the €2 fee is non-refundable, even if the declaration is invalidated. Items are counted by tariff classification (HS code), not by quantity: five T-shirts count as one item only if they all have exactly the same HS code.

Since 1 November 2026, a line-item product identifier is mandatory on EU customs declarations for distance sales of imported goods with an intrinsic value of €150 or less. Product identifiers also help identify exactly which items are being returned and provide a source for the refund.
